Piotroski F-score
What Piotroski F-score measures

Measures. Nine yes/no tests of whether the accounts improved on last year — profitability, cash generation, debt, dilution and efficiency. Each passed test scores one point, so the range is 0 to 9.

What would make you look closer. The total matters less than WHICH tests failed. Two failures that share one underlying cause (a working-capital swing usually fails several at once) are one event, not several. Read the failed rows.

What moves it. Year-on-year change, not level: a consistently strong company that did not improve scores badly, and a weak one that improved from a low base scores well. Every input is backward-looking, so it reflects the accounts as filed, not the business as it is today.

What it means for the business. Each point is a specific thing the business did differently from last year: earned a profit, converted it to cash, carried less debt, diluted less, turned assets faster, held margin. A high total means most of those moved the same way in one year — which for a cyclical usually means the year sat at a different point in the cycle from the one before it, not that nine separate things independently improved. A low total means the same machinery ran the other way. The score is a count of directional changes, so it says what happened between two balance sheets and nothing about the level either one sits at.

Piotroski (2000), Journal of Accounting Research

Altman Z (original, manufacturer, uses market cap)
What Altman Z-score measures

Measures. A bankruptcy-risk score built by weighting five balance-sheet and earnings ratios. Above roughly 2.6 (Z'' variant) sits outside the historical distress zone; below about 1.1 sits inside it.

What would make you look closer. Only the distress end carries information. A reading well above the safe line is not 'safer' than one just above it — the model cannot tell those apart and was never built to.

What moves it. Heavily by leverage and retained earnings. A company with little or no debt produces a very high score almost automatically, because the equity-to-liabilities term dominates the total.

What it means for the business. Inside the distress zone, the arithmetic is saying the business has little retained earnings and working capital relative to what it owes — so it depends on refinancing or on operations turning cash quickly, because there is not much accumulated buffer to draw on. Outside the zone, the same terms say the opposite mechanically: accumulated earnings and working capital are large next to liabilities, so a bad year is absorbed by the balance sheet rather than by a lender. The model was fitted on manufacturers heading into bankruptcy, so it is describing that specific mechanism and no other.

What Beneish M-score measures

Measures. Eight ratios combined into one number that flags accounting patterns statistically associated with earnings manipulation. Above −1.78 is the published threshold at which the model flags a firm as manipulation-like.

What would make you look closer. Above the threshold is a prompt to read which of the eight terms produced it — never an accusation. Most companies above the line are not manipulating anything. Below the line is not clearance either: the model misses plenty.

What moves it. ACQUISITIONS TRIP IT MECHANICALLY. Buying a business inflates AQI (goodwill and intangibles land on the balance sheet) and SGI (revenue jumps) with nobody doing anything wrong. It was fitted on companies manipulating organically, and on larger firms than these.

What it means for the business. Above the threshold, at least some of the eight ratios are moving the way they move when revenue is recognised earlier, costs are capitalised rather than expensed, or reserves are released — but the identical ratio movements are produced by a genuine change in the business: entering a slower-paying customer segment moves DSRI, an acquisition moves AQI and LVGI together, a price rise moves GMI. The score cannot separate those, which is why the term breakdown matters more than the total. Below the threshold means those ratios are not moving in that combination this year, which is not evidence about any other year.

Accruals ratio
What Accruals ratio measures

Measures. How much of reported profit is NOT backed by cash: net income minus operating cash flow, divided by total assets.

What would make you look closer. A high positive number means profit is not turning into cash — worth asking which working-capital line explains the gap. Roughly above 0.10 is the level at which most readers would go looking.

What moves it. Working-capital movements above all. Growth consumes cash in any business with a long cash cycle, so a fast-growing year can show high accruals for entirely structural reasons. Scaling by total assets also means an acquisition dilutes the ratio.

What it means for the business. Above roughly 0.10, the business is reporting profit it has not yet collected as cash: the earnings exist as receivables, inventory or capitalised cost on the balance sheet rather than as money received. Mechanically, that profit now depends on those balances converting — customers paying, stock selling. Below zero the relationship runs the other way: the business collected more cash than it booked as profit, which is what happens when working capital is being released or when cash was received for revenue recognised in an earlier period. Neither direction is sustainable indefinitely; both reverse.

Operating cash flow / net income
What Operating cash flow / net income measures

Measures. How many dollars of operating cash the business generated per dollar of reported profit. Around 1.0x means the two agree.

What would make you look closer. Sustained readings below about 0.8x are the thing to check — one year says very little on its own. Look across four or five years before reading anything into a single figure.

What moves it. The working-capital cycle, which in commodity businesses makes it oscillate violently in both directions for structural reasons. Repeated divergence BOTH ways usually indicates a cycle; persistent one-way divergence is the pattern worth reading about.

What it means for the business. Below about 1.0x, some of the reported profit is sitting in working capital rather than in the bank — the business has to fund the difference from cash reserves, a revolver or new borrowing, because the profit is not yet available to spend. Above 1.0x, cash is coming in faster than profit is being booked, which is the mechanical signature of working capital unwinding or of heavy depreciation on assets bought in earlier years. In a business with a long cash cycle, the ratio swings both ways as a matter of course, which is why the several-year pattern carries the information and one year does not.

Days sales outstanding
What Days sales outstanding measures

Measures. Average days between making a sale and collecting the cash.

What would make you look closer. Rising faster than revenue is the classic sign of looser payment terms or a collection problem — worth reading the receivables note and any change in stated terms.

What moves it. Customer mix and contract terms. A shift toward larger customers with more negotiating power lengthens it without anything going wrong.

What it means for the business. Days sales outstanding is how long customers take to pay. Rising DSO means cash arrives later relative to when revenue is booked, so the business is funding customers for longer and the receivable balance grows for the same sales. That happens when terms are extended to win volume, when the customer mix shifts toward slower payers, or when specific accounts stop paying. Falling DSO releases cash from the receivable balance. The number cannot distinguish those causes; the receivables note and the MD&A can.

What Days inventory outstanding measures

Measures. Average days stock sits before being sold.

What would make you look closer. Rising without matching revenue growth is worth checking against obsolescence provisions — and worth splitting between raw materials and finished goods, which tell different stories.

Days payable outstanding
What Days payable outstanding measures

Measures. Average days the company takes to pay its own suppliers.

What would make you look closer. A sharp rise can flatter operating cash flow for a year — worth checking the cash flow statement's working-capital movements to see how much of the year's cash came from stretching suppliers.

What moves it. Negotiated terms and supplier relationships; also simply when the year-end fell relative to a payment run.

Goodwill + intangibles / assets
What Goodwill and intangibles / assets measures

Measures. How much of the balance sheet is made up of assets with no physical form — mostly the premium paid in acquisitions.

What would make you look closer. Above roughly a third of assets, the impairment testing assumptions in the notes — headroom and discount rate — become worth reading, because that is where a write-down would originate.

What moves it. Acquisition history, almost entirely. A company that has never bought anything will sit near zero regardless of quality.

What it means for the business. This is the share of the balance sheet that is purchase price paid above the net assets acquired, rather than anything with a separate resale value. Mechanically, a high share means book equity depends on acquired businesses continuing to perform as assumed when they were bought: if they do not, the write-down reduces equity without any cash leaving, which can breach covenants written against equity or leverage. A low share means book equity is backed mostly by assets that exist independently of an acquisition assumption.

Interest coverage
What Interest coverage measures

Measures. How many times over operating profit covers the interest bill.

What would make you look closer. Below about 3x, the debt maturity schedule and whether rates reset become worth reading. Very high figures simply mean there is little debt.

What moves it. The amount of debt and the rate on it. It says nothing about whether the debt is well used.

What it means for the business. Coverage is how many times over operating profit pays the interest bill. It is the direct measure of how far profit can fall before interest has to be paid from cash reserves or new borrowing rather than from earnings. A low ratio means that headroom is small in arithmetic terms, and it is also what most credit agreements are written against — so the covenant, not the lender's opinion, is what usually acts first. A high ratio means operating profit could fall a long way before interest became the binding constraint.
