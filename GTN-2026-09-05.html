The full background — what it sells, who buys it, how it makes money

Gray Media owns and operates local television stations that broadcast entertainment and news programming affiliated with major networks, and operates digital media properties.

What it sellsBroadcast television advertising (local, regional and national), retransmission consent fees (fees paid by cable, satellite and phone video distributors for the right to carry stations' signals), and digital advertising through Gray Digital Media, a digital agency. Also operates video production companies including Raycom Sports, Tupelo Media Group, and PowerNation Studios, and studio production facilities Assembly Atlanta and Third Rail Studios.
Who buys itAdvertisers (local, regional and national), including the services sector (financial, legal and medical), automotive industry, and political candidates and organizations. Video programmers and multichannel video programming distributors (cable system operators, telephone video distributors, direct broadcast satellite operators). No single advertising customer represented more than 5% of broadcast advertising revenue in 2025, 2024 or 2023. The filing states the company serves end markets dependent on cyclical economic conditions and political election cycles.
How it makes moneyRevenue is derived primarily from selling broadcast advertising time during network and locally-produced programming, from retransmission consent fees paid by video distributors for signal carriage, and from digital advertising. Stations retain most advertising revenue from non-network programming time and a small portion of advertising time within network programming (the networks retain the majority). Main input costs include programming fees from broadcast networks and syndicated programs, and personnel costs.
SegmentsThe filing does not describe reporting segments; it appears the company reports as a single segment.
WhereOperates television stations in 114 full-power television markets across the United States, serving approximately 37% of U.S. television households. Markets range from DMA 7 (Atlanta, Georgia) to DMA 209 (North Platte, Nebraska). Headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia.

Scale, as stated in the filing: 114 full-power television markets; 77 markets with top-rated television station (Nielsen rank #1); 97 markets with first and/or second highest rated television station; 47 Telemundo-affiliated markets totaling over 1.6 million Hispanic TV Households; 37% of U.S. television households reached; $3.1 billion revenue in 2025.

Industry context

Broadcast television is in a structurally declining linear-viewership, cord-cutting environment: cable penetration has fallen to roughly 32-34% of US TV households in 2026, with both traditional pay-TV and virtual MVPDs (YouTube TV, Sling, Hulu/Fubo) losing subscribers even during football season. Station groups including Gray, Nexstar, Sinclair and Scripps have offset this with retransmission-consent fee increases and periodic blackouts/carriage disputes with distributors, a dynamic industry observers expect to become more common. The sector's biggest 2026 regulatory event was the FCC's August 6, 2026 party-line (2-1) repeal of the 39% national audience-reach ownership cap in place since 2004, replacing it with case-by-case review — a move expected to spur station-group consolidation but facing threatened litigation from Free Press and objections from Democratic FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez and congressional Democrats who argue only Congress can change the statutory cap. Separately, large-scale consolidation attempts (Nexstar-Tegna, Sinclair-Scripps) have struggled to close due to deal-structure and control issues, per CNBC reporting. 2026 is a midterm election year, giving broadcasters (including Gray, whose footprint covers all competitive Senate and gubernatorial races per the Cook Political Report) an above-normal but temporary political-advertising tailwind that inflates current-year results relative to the underlying core-advertising trend.

Piotroski F-score
What Piotroski F-score measures

Measures. Nine yes/no tests of whether the accounts improved on last year — profitability, cash generation, debt, dilution and efficiency. Each passed test scores one point, so the range is 0 to 9.

What would make you look closer. The total matters less than WHICH tests failed. Two failures that share one underlying cause (a working-capital swing usually fails several at once) are one event, not several. Read the failed rows.

What moves it. Year-on-year change, not level: a consistently strong company that did not improve scores badly, and a weak one that improved from a low base scores well. Every input is backward-looking, so it reflects the accounts as filed, not the business as it is today.

What it means for the business. Each point is a specific thing the business did differently from last year: earned a profit, converted it to cash, carried less debt, diluted less, turned assets faster, held margin. A high total means most of those moved the same way in one year — which for a cyclical usually means the year sat at a different point in the cycle from the one before it, not that nine separate things independently improved. A low total means the same machinery ran the other way. The score is a count of directional changes, so it says what happened between two balance sheets and nothing about the level either one sits at.

What Altman Z-score measures

Measures. A bankruptcy-risk score built by weighting five balance-sheet and earnings ratios. Above roughly 2.6 (Z'' variant) sits outside the historical distress zone; below about 1.1 sits inside it.

What would make you look closer. Only the distress end carries information. A reading well above the safe line is not 'safer' than one just above it — the model cannot tell those apart and was never built to.

What moves it. Heavily by leverage and retained earnings. A company with little or no debt produces a very high score almost automatically, because the equity-to-liabilities term dominates the total.

Accruals ratio
What Accruals ratio measures

Measures. How much of reported profit is NOT backed by cash: net income minus operating cash flow, divided by total assets.

What would make you look closer. A high positive number means profit is not turning into cash — worth asking which working-capital line explains the gap. Roughly above 0.10 is the level at which most readers would go looking.

What moves it. Working-capital movements above all. Growth consumes cash in any business with a long cash cycle, so a fast-growing year can show high accruals for entirely structural reasons. Scaling by total assets also means an acquisition dilutes the ratio.

What it means for the business. Above roughly 0.10, the business is reporting profit it has not yet collected as cash: the earnings exist as receivables, inventory or capitalised cost on the balance sheet rather than as money received. Mechanically, that profit now depends on those balances converting — customers paying, stock selling. Below zero the relationship runs the other way: the business collected more cash than it booked as profit, which is what happens when working capital is being released or when cash was received for revenue recognised in an earlier period. Neither direction is sustainable indefinitely; both reverse.
